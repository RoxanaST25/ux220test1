From Physics Research to the World Wide Web

The original purpose: sharing scientific information

Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989 while working at CERN, a particle physics research laboratory. Scientists at different universities and laboratories needed to exchange information. His proposal addressed how to organise and link information across different computer systems. The Web supported physics research by making documents easier to find and share; it was an information-management solution rather than a new theory of physics.

The Web today: everyday activities

Today, people use websites to study, read news, shop, access public services and communicate. Web applications also support entertainment, banking and collaborative work. The audience now extends far beyond scientists, and many pages let people create content or complete transactions rather than simply read documents. Scientific collaboration remains one of these uses.

What has stayed the same, and what has changed?

Both the original Web and the modern Web connect people with information through linked resources. A scientist following a link to a research document and a student opening an online lesson use the same basic idea. The main differences are the audience, purpose and level of interaction. The early project served a research community, whereas the modern Web supports public, personal and commercial activities. This broader use brings benefits such as easier access to learning, alongside challenges involving privacy, misinformation and unequal access.
